The Future of Airline Merchandising for OTAs

Airline retailing expands the merchandising surface. When the unit of sale turns from a fare into an offer, the OTA flight shelf stops being a price list and becomes a store.

01

How airline retailing is changing the OTA flight shelf

For years, an OTA's flight shelf held one thing: a fare. A price, a route, a time, maybe a baggage note buried in the fine print. The traveller compared numbers and picked the cheapest one that looked bearable.

That shelf is changing shape. Airlines now want to sell the way retailers sell, with bundles, seat upgrades, flexible tickets, lounge passes, Wi-Fi, priority boarding and offers built for one traveller at one moment. Every one of those items needs a place to be seen, explained and chosen.

The question for OTAs is simple. When the airline turns into a retailer, who runs the store?

02

How NDC is shifting airline distribution from fares to offers

The old distribution model was built around filed fares. Airlines published prices, intermediaries resold them, and anything extra was sold later, usually on the airline's own website or at the airport.

Modern airline retailing flips that logic. Standards like IATA's NDC let airlines send rich, structured offers straight into third-party channels. Offers can carry images, seat maps, bundle descriptions and ancillaries, priced for the context of the request. The move toward Offers and Orders, including ONE Order, goes further and aims to replace the PNR and ticket with a single retail-style order record.

The result is that the unit of sale is no longer a fare. It is an offer. And an offer can be assembled, compared, ranked and presented in far more ways than a fare ever could.

03

OTAs are becoming airline merchandisers

When offers get richer, the role of the OTA shifts. It is no longer just a search engine that sorts flights by price. It becomes the place where an airline's product is staged, explained and sold.

That is merchandising. It means deciding which offer appears first, how a bundle is described, when an upgrade is suggested and how two carriers' very different products sit side by side in a way a traveller can actually compare. A Basic fare and a Flex bundle are not the same product, even when they fly the same plane.

Some OTAs will treat rich content as decoration, a few extra icons on a results page. Others will treat it as inventory to be curated. The second group will own the traveller's attention, and with it, the margin that comes from everything sold beyond the seat.

04

Where the opportunity sits

At Techspian, we see rich offers opening three doors for OTAs: ranking, personalization and ancillaries. Each one turns more data into a better decision for the traveller.

Ranking. Sorting by lowest price made sense when every result looked alike. With rich offers, the cheapest option is often not the best value. A fare that includes a checked bag and a free change can beat a cheaper fare once the extras are added. Ranking can now weigh total trip cost, included benefits, schedule quality and fit with what the traveller searched for. The OTA that ranks for value, not just price, earns trust.

Personalization. Rich offers are only useful when the right one reaches the right person. A business traveller on a same-day return cares about flexibility and seat choice. A family on a holiday cares about bags and sitting together. Using search context, past behaviour and loyalty signals, an OTA can shape what each traveller sees first, and request offers from airlines that reflect that context.

Ancillaries. Seats, bags, meals, lounge access and flexibility used to be sold after booking, if at all. With structured offers, they can be shown and priced at the moment of choice, inside the booking flow, and serviced later through the same order. That is a revenue line many OTAs have barely touched.

05

The technology OTAs need to sell rich airline offers

Merchandising looks like a front-end problem. Most of it is engineering underneath. To sell rich offers well, an OTA needs a few things working together:

  • Normalized content. Every airline describes its bundles and ancillaries differently. Those descriptions have to be mapped into a common structure so they can be compared fairly.
  • A ranking layer. Logic that scores offers on value, relevance and commercial goals, and can be tested and tuned without a full release.
  • Context-aware offer requests. Search calls that pass the right signals to airlines, so the offers coming back are already shaped for the traveller.
  • Order servicing. Changes, refunds and add-ons after booking have to work on modern orders, not just legacy tickets, or the experience breaks at the worst possible moment.
  • Measurement. Attach rates, conversion by offer type and revenue per booking, tracked closely enough to know what to show next time.

None of this is a single integration. It is a capability, built and refined over time.

06

The future of airline merchandising belongs to OTAs

Airlines are building the products. Travellers are ready to buy more than a seat. What sits between them is the shelf, and OTAs are the ones holding it.

The OTAs that win the next phase of air distribution will not be the ones with the lowest fare on the page. They will be the ones that understand what they are selling, rank it honestly, shape it for the person in front of them and make the extras easy to say yes to.

The merchandising surface has grown. The real question is how much of it OTAs are ready to use.
